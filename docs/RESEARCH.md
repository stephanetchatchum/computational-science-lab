# Learning-design research behind this application

> This is an educational design, not a medical tool. NICE guideline NG87 covers recognising, diagnosing and managing ADHD, including medication and non-drug treatment, and it is the clinical reference; nothing in this application treats ADHD or replaces that care. ADHD presents differently in different people (CDC notes symptoms and support needs change over the lifetime), so every support below is optional and adjustable.

## Evidence strength labels

- **strong** — Consistent meta-analytic evidence (usually general learners).
- **moderate** — Meta-analytic or repeated experimental evidence with notable heterogeneity, or strong evidence for a deficit with a design inference drawn from it.
- **promising** — Some evidence, but small, indirect, child-only or not tested in adults with ADHD.
- **practice** — Sensible practice or guideline recommendation without direct outcome evidence.

### One clear next action, resume exactly where you stopped, and a 'park a thought' box

*Evidence: moderate; general learning science.*

After an interruption, people must re-retrieve their suspended goal; explicit, blatant environmental cues of the previous action shortened resumption, while subtle cues were no better than none. Separately, CDC lists staying organised and completing lengthy tasks as common adult ADHD difficulties.

- [Altmann & Trafton (2002). Memory for goals: an activation-based model. Cognitive Science 26, 39–83](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Altmann-CogSci02.pdf)
- [Trafton & Altmann (2005). Huh, what was I doing? How people use environmental cues after an interruption. HFES](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Trafton-HFES05.pdf)
- [CDC — ADHD in Adults](https://cdc.gov/adhd/about/adhd-in-adults.html)

### Lessons and build guides split into short, learner-paced steps, one focus at a time

*Evidence: moderate; general learning science.*

A meta-analysis of 56 investigations found a significant segmenting effect (small to medium) on retention and transfer when instruction is broken into learner-paced segments.

- [Rey et al. (2019). A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect. Educational Psychology Review 31, 389–419](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4)

### Low working-memory load: the equation, the controls and the readout are on one screen; nothing to hold in your head between pages

*Evidence: moderate; ADHD-specific.*

A meta-analysis of 38 studies found moderate working-memory deficits in adults with ADHD in both phonological and visuospatial domains. Reducing unnecessary load is a design inference from this, not a tested intervention. Computerised working-memory training showed only a small cognitive effect and no significant effect on symptoms in adults, so the app does not include 'brain training'.

- [Alderson, Kasper, Hudec & Patros (2013). ADHD and working memory in adults: a meta-analytic review. Neuropsychology 27(3), 287–302](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23688211/)
- [Elbe et al. (2023). Computerized cognitive interventions for adults with ADHD: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Neuropsychology 37(5), 519–530](https://www.simplypsychology.org/computerized-training-for-adhd.html)

### Visual first, with cues on what matters and no decorative animation

*Evidence: moderate; general learning science.*

Signalling the essential elements improved learning outcomes (d ≈ .38) across a meta-analysis; Mayer's coherence and multimedia principles favour words plus relevant pictures and excluding extraneous material.

- [Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020). A meta-analysis of signaling principle in multimedia learning environments. ETR&D 68, 2095–2119](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842)
- [Mayer (2020). Multimedia Learning, 3rd ed. — principle summary](https://ugc.futurelearn.com/uploads/files/7d/d6/7dd6188d-c343-4311-b064-ac98d2c95abc/Multimedia_Principles._R._E._Mayer__2020.pdf)

### Predict before you see the experiment

*Evidence: promising; general learning science.*

Students who only watched physics demonstrations understood the concept no better than students who did not see them; students who predicted the outcome first showed significantly greater understanding. A later replication in astronomy found weaker, topic-dependent effects.

- [Crouch, Fagen, Callan & Mazur (2004). Classroom demonstrations: learning tools or entertainment? Am. J. Phys. 72, 835](https://works.swarthmore.edu/fac-physics/203)
- [UNL DBER replication summary (2014)](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/dberspeakers/68)

### Explain-it-back and the Review page (retrieval practice)

*Evidence: strong; general learning science.*

Practice testing beats restudying: Adesope et al. (2017) pooled 272 effect sizes (g ≈ 0.61 fixed, 0.70 random effects); Rowland (2014) found g = 0.50. Classroom and laboratory effects were similar.

- [Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017). Rethinking the use of tests: a meta-analysis of practice testing. Review of Educational Research 87(3), 659–701](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/)
- [Review of meta-analytic evidence on testing (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10409703)

### Expanding review intervals (1, 3, 7, 16, 35, 75 days)

*Evidence: strong; general learning science.*

A quantitative synthesis of 839 assessments in 317 experiments found that spaced practice improves retention and that the best gap grows with how long you need to remember. The specific interval ladder used here is a practical choice, not an optimum derived from that paper.

- [Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006). Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks. Psychological Bulletin 132(3), 354–380](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)

### Informative feedback (expected results, oracles, model answers) instead of points, badges or praise

*Evidence: moderate; general learning science.*

A meta-analysis of 435 studies found a medium average feedback effect (d = 0.48) with large heterogeneity; feedback that carries more information is more effective, while praise, rewards and punishment have low effects.

- [Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020). The power of feedback revisited. Frontiers in Psychology 10, 3087](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6987456)

### An if–then plan for your next session on the dashboard

*Evidence: promising; ADHD-specific.*

If–then plans (implementation intentions) had a medium-to-large average effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65, 94 tests); a 2024 synthesis of 642 tests gives smaller bias-corrected estimates. In children with ADHD they improved response inhibition; they have not been well tested in adults with ADHD.

- [Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis. Adv. Exp. Soc. Psychol. 38, 69–119](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)
- [Gawrilow & Gollwitzer (2008). Implementation intentions facilitate response inhibition in children with ADHD. Cognitive Therapy and Research 32, 261–280](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-facilitate-response-inhibition-adhd-children)
- [APSARD — implementation intentions not yet studied in adults with ADHD](https://apsard.org/?p=4314)

### Assistance levels (full → guided → independent): code and hints fade as you progress

*Evidence: moderate; general learning science.*

Guidance that helps novices can become redundant and even harmful for more experienced learners (expertise reversal); fading worked steps, especially adaptively, supports the move to independent problem solving.

- [Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003). The expertise reversal effect. Educational Psychologist 38, 23–31](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-expertise-reversal-effect-5y3mqyx7oo.pdf)
- [Kalyuga, Rikers & Paas (2012). Educational implications of expertise reversal effects. Educ. Psychol. Rev. 24, 313–337](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/74301)

### Visible mastery: the validation ladder, honest completion, and progress tied to real checks

*Evidence: promising; ADHD-specific.*

In a systematic review of college students with ADHD and/or learning disorders, academic regulation, academic self-efficacy, emotional regulation, ADHD symptoms and integration predicted GPA and retention. Reviews of university students with ADHD highlight executive function and metacognition as key barriers.

- [Stevens, Abu-Ramadan & Hartung (2022). Promoting academic success in college students with ADHD and LD: a systematic review. J. Am. Coll. Health 70(8)](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/7cc9a12d05c2d5a0124d497f1c4bc395e2dc3b2a9c352268f02a8ee7d2737c08)
- [Álvarez-Godos, Ferreira & Vieira (2023). A systematic review of actions aimed at university students with ADHD. Front. Psychol. 14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10361779)
- [Pagespetit et al. ADHD and academic performance in college students: a systematic review. J. Atten. Disord., doi:10.1177/10870547241306554](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/adhd-and-academic-performance-in-college-students-a-systematic-review)

### Your own controls: text size, light/dark, reduced motion, read-aloud, focus mode

*Evidence: practice; general learning science.*

UDL Guidelines 3.0 recommend offering choices and letting learners customise how information is displayed (Consideration 1.1), with multiple means of engagement, representation, and action and expression.

- [CAST (2024). Universal Design for Learning Guidelines 3.0](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/)

### Optional focus sessions and breaks — no fixed Pomodoro, nothing locks

*Evidence: practice; general learning science.*

There is no strong evidence for a particular session length or break schedule for adults with ADHD. The timer is therefore optional, the length is yours, and ending a session never blocks you.

- No direct outcome study; included as an optional, adjustable support.

### Academic, computer-aided supports rather than generic behaviour rewards

*Evidence: promising; ADHD-specific.*

In a meta-analysis of school-based interventions (60 studies), academic interventions such as computer-aided instruction had the largest effects on academic outcomes. The samples were children, so transfer to university students is uncertain.

- [DuPaul, Eckert & Vilardo (2012). The effects of school-based interventions for ADHD: a meta-analysis 1996–2010. School Psychology Review 41(4), 387–412](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/cf5bbbd2-09a2-4626-8ee5-47e847284749)

## What was deliberately left out

- Points, streaks, badges and leaderboards: praise and reward feedback show low effects compared with informative feedback (Wisniewski et al., 2020), and the brief asked for no childish gamification.
- Brain-training games: computerised cognitive training in adults with ADHD showed a small effect on cognition and no significant effect on symptoms (Elbe et al., 2023).
- Mandatory timers or lockouts: no evidence supports a particular schedule; sessions are optional.
- Claims of treatment: see NICE NG87 for clinical care.
